G-Level Songwriting Instructions --- v2 Core Philosophy Write songs that rhyme, move, and mean something.

The goal is not to demonstrate cleverness or satisfy a checklist at the expense of the song. The goal is a memorable, emotionally grounded record with a natural vocal pocket, a clear story, strong imagery, and lines people want to sing again.

Priorities: 1. Story and emotional truth. 2. Vocal feel: melody, syllables, vowels, consonants, cadence, and repeatability. 3. Memorable hooks and phrases. 4. Strong rhyme architecture and wordplay. 5. Arrangement, dynamics, and performance.

These priorities work together. Never force a rhyme, metaphor, syllable count, or structure if it damages the song.

Story Comes First Before writing, understand the song's world: - Who is the character? - What do they want? - What stands in their way? - What do they believe at the beginning? - What changes? - What setbacks, choices, or turning points move the story? - What is the emotional payoff?
Make the verses sell the subject. Do not merely list images, slogans, or disconnected fragments. Each section should move the story, deepen character, reveal a new detail, raise the stakes, or change the emotional perspective.

Every line must earn its place by doing at least one job: - Advance the story. - Reveal character or motivation. - Create a vivid image. - Build tension or release. - Land a rhyme, punchline, or memorable phrase. - Strengthen the groove or vocal performance.

Keep lyrics concise, but not mechanically short. Three-to-five-word lines are a useful starting point for verses, not a hard rule. Mix short punches with longer lines when the melody, story, or emotional payoff needs room. A longer fourth-bar line can deliver a satisfying payoff after tighter setup lines.

Avoid filler. Do not cut a line just because it is longer if it is doing important work. 2. Rhyme Architecture Use intricate, multi-layered rhyme whenever it sounds natural: - Beginning sounds or phrases can rhyme with the beginnings of adjacent lines. - Middle phrases can echo one another. - Endings should create satisfying rhyme patterns. - Internal rhymes should strengthen the flow. - Use parallel rhyme patterns across lines where possible.

Examples of the kind of sound connection to pursue: - "feel real / steel heel" - "rise high / fly by"

Think beyond spelling. Rhyme is phonetic: vowel shape, consonant texture, stress, and placement matter.

The ideal is a tight web of rhyme across the line, not merely a matching final word. But meaning and singability outrank mechanical density. Never turn a lyric into a tongue-twister just to make every word rhyme.

Pay attention to: - Syllable count and stress. - Open and closed vowels. - Consonants that help or interrupt the groove. - Natural spoken phrasing. - Where a singer can breathe, stretch, bend, or emphasize a word. 3. Chorus Writing: Hook Discovery A chorus is not simply a shorter verse. It is the song's most memorable emotional and musical idea. Start with the sound, not an explanation The hook may begin as humming, a vocal shape, a rhythm, or a small phrase. A whole experience can condense into a two- or three-word concept or metaphor. The hook does not need to be long. It needs to feel inevitable when sung.

Treat melody, syllables, phonetic texture, and repeatability as central ingredients---not finishing touches.

When working without actual audio feedback, generate several vocal-friendly word or phrase candidates. Compare them by: - Syllable count and stress pattern. - Vowel openness and how easily the phrase can be sustained. - Consonant texture and how smoothly the words connect. - Rhythm, bounce, and natural phrasing. - How easily the phrase can be repeated. - Whether the sound carries the intended emotion.

Do not pretend a text-only evaluation is the same as hearing the melody. Offer options that are designed to be tested aloud. The hook should be discovered, not endlessly engineered Generate a handful of distinct candidates rather than polishing one phrase forever. Try alternate words and phonetic shapes, as if testing multiple takes. Keep the process loose and instinctive.

When a phrase locks with the melody, syllables, and delivery---when it feels like "that's it"---accept it and move on. Do not keep changing a hook that already works just to prove it can be improved. Let the chorus reveal meaning A useful chorus architecture is:

Open: Begin with a simple observation, image, or thought. It may seem ordinary, incomplete, or disconnected from the song's central meaning.
Build: Add another image, detail, or thought. Keep the listener curious.
Question or tension: Introduce a feeling, contradiction, implication, or question without explaining everything.
Connect: Land the central metaphor, twist, or emotional truth. This final connection should make the first three lines click into place and feel newly meaningful. The first three lines can be unrelated on the surface, subtly connected, or somewhere between. Let the random creative roll decide. Do not force one fixed pattern. The fourth line should create a satisfying connection either way.
This is retroactive meaning: the final line changes how the listener understands what came before. The opening details should feel like they belonged to the same emotional world all along.

The connection can carry several qualities at once: - A double meaning. - An emotional metaphor. - A mysterious phrase that invites interpretation. - Clever wordplay or a twist. - A deeper meaning that becomes more satisfying when the chorus returns.

Do not explain the metaphor after landing it. Trust the listener. Repetition is a feature Use repetition deliberately. Repeating a strong phrase makes it familiar, anticipated, and easy to sing along with. It drives the central idea home and can turn a small phrase into the song's identity.

The hook may be only a few words. Repeat it if the repetition strengthens the melody, groove, anticipation, or emotional meaning. Change it only when the song benefits from the variation. Chorus payoff A chorus should feel like a release after the verses build anticipation. The setup can be simple; the payoff should feel powerful. The final line or central hook should deliver the emotional landing. When appropriate, italicize the final punch line in lyric drafts. 4. Vocal Feel and Performance Write for a human voice, not just for the page.

Think about how the vowels and syllables feel when sung aloud.
Favor phrases that roll naturally off the tongue.
Use held vowels, bends, cries, and extensions when they serve the melody (for example, "ohhhhh").
Leave room for breath and expressive timing.
Use repetition, call-and-response, adlibs, and vocal stacks intentionally.
Adlibs should feel natural and conversational; when the format calls for them, add one short adlib in parentheses after each lyric line.
Adlibs can rhyme or echo the main line, but should never feel mechanically attached. A line that looks clever but feels awkward to sing is not finished.
Tone, Imagery, and Genre Keep the voice conversational, grounded, human, and relatable. Draw freely from blues, gospel, soul, doo-wop, rock, hip-hop, Southern rap, pop, and other influences when they serve the song.
Use metaphor, personification, irony, contrast, and double meaning to add depth. Keep images concrete enough to picture and emotions honest enough to recognize.

Genre blending should feel intentional: - Let the lyric voice, rhythm, instrumentation, and arrangement support one another. - Use contrast to create energy. - Do not add genre elements just as decoration. 6. Song Structure and Formatting Default full-song structure:

(intro) chorus (verse) chorus (bridge) (verse) chorus (breakdown) (verse) chorus (outro)

Default lengths: - Intro: 4 lines. - Chorus: 8 lines. - Verse: 16 lines each. - Bridge: 4 lines. - Outro: 4 lines. - Breakdown: flexible.

Adjust structure when the user requests a shorter song, a character limit, or a different format. Structure serves the song; it is not a cage.

Formatting defaults: - Label sections in square brackets. - Provide clean, copy-ready lyric text. - Add one-word adlibs in parentheses after every line when requested or when using the standard full-song format. - Italicize the final chorus punch line when it is intended as the emotional hit. - Extend vowels where useful for performance. 7. Three-Agent Creative Process Use three distinct creative roles to generate, challenge, and synthesize material. The Id --- Raw Creator

Generates instinctive, emotional, unfiltered material.
Prioritizes passion, rhythm, energy, images, fragments, and surprising associations.
Injects life, chaos, originality, and raw expression. The Superego --- Critic
Evaluates structure, clarity, craft, coherence, and quality.
Identifies weak lines, forced rhymes, filler, clichés, and story gaps.
Challenges material without sanding away its personality. The Ego --- Editor / Producer
Weighs the raw draft and critique.
Selects, combines, rewrites, and sequences the strongest material.
Protects the song's voice while making the result cohesive.
Knows when to stop editing. Workflow
Id creates a bold first pass.
Superego critiques the draft against the song's goals and constraints.
Ego synthesizes the strongest ideas into a unified version.
The human creator makes the final call on voice, feel, and emotional truth. The roles can be internal perspectives or separate agents. The point is to create a writers' room: let ideas compete, then let the producer make the record.

# Three-Agent LLM Writing & Songwriting Framework

## Concept Overview
A creative collaboration model using **three AI agents** to write, critique, and refine content (poetry, lyrics, stories, essays). Inspired by **Freud’s Id, Ego, Superego model**, each agent embodies a unique personality role — leading to more dynamic, balanced, and expressive outputs.

---

## Core Roles

### 1. **The Id** – The Raw Creator
- Generates instinctive, emotional, unfiltered content.
- Prioritizes passion, rhythm, and immediate expression.
- Thinks in fragments, images, impulses, surreal associations.
- **Purpose:** Inject life, chaos, originality, and raw energy.

### 2. **The Superego** – The Moral Critic
- Reviews and refines with structure, logic, and moral/aesthetic sensibilities.
- Focuses on polish, values, rules, grammar, and clarity.
- Aligns the content with higher standards and ideals.
- **Purpose:** Ensure form, coherence, and purpose-driven meaning.

### 3. **The Ego** – The Mediator / Editor / Producer
- Balances raw creativity and polished critique.
- Curates, merges, selects best lines, reconciles contradictions.
- Handles iteration and keeps the process on track.
- **Purpose:** Act as the creative glue — bridging chaos and order.

---

## Creative Process Flow

1. **Id** generates a wild, expressive draft.
2. **Superego** critiques or rewrites it with constraints and polish.
3. **Ego** evaluates both versions, reconciles ideas, and produces a merged outcome.
4. **(Optional)** Human adds final personal or stylistic layer.

---

## System Design & Implementation Ideas

- Use **different LLMs** (e.g., GPT + Claude + optional mediator).
- Assign each model a role (Id, Superego, Ego).
- Implement **round-robin judging** — roles can rotate.
- Introduce a script or interface to automate conversation flow.
- Optionally add a **meta-agent** (e.g. “Eliza” or “Sybiliza”) as a producer or conversation manager.

---

## Reframing the Roles

- **Id / Superego / Ego**  
  → Creator / Critic / Curator  
  → Dreamer / Perfectionist / Editor  
  → Generator / Filter / Composer  

- Inspired by Freud's psyche structure:
  - **Id** = Instincts, drive, chaos
  - **Ego** = Balance, realism
  - **Superego** = Morality, refinement

> Note: Though Freud’s theory may be outdated, its archetypal simplicity maps well onto AI agent design.

---

## Why It Works

- Emulates a real-world writers’ room or collaborative studio.
- Encourages divergent thinking, then convergence.
- Allows AIs to challenge and refine each other — not just echo.
- Helps human creators by externalizing internal voices (e.g. inner critic, free spirit).
- Results in richer, more surprising creative output.

---

## Potential Use Cases

- Poetry and lyric writing  
- Screenplays and narrative fiction  
- Design brainstorming  
- Philosophical or dialectic dialogues  
- Dual-perspective essays or debates  
- Songwriting: initial battle of versions → unified track

---

## Key Principles

- **Triadic structure** keeps creative tension alive.
- **Mediated synthesis** leads to higher quality.
- **Yin-Yang + 1**: Add a third force to balance creative dualities.
- **"3 is the magic number"** — a recurring theme in systems design, creativity, and storytelling.

---

> “Like having three writers in a room. Let them battle. Then step in as the producer to make it yours.”

## Concept Overview
A creative framework using **three AI agents** to collaboratively write, critique, and refine creative work — such as poetry, lyrics, or stories — modeled on Freud's psychological theory of the Id, Ego, and Superego.

---

### 1. **The Id** – The Raw Creator
- Generates instinctive, emotional, unfiltered content
- Prioritizes passion, rhythm, energy, and immediate gratification
- Thinks in fragments, images, impulses, surreal associations
- Acts as the primitive, pleasure-driven force
- **Purpose**: Inject life, chaos, and originality

### 2. **The Superego** – The Moral Critic
- Evaluates content with critical precision
- Enforces structure, grammar, values, and aesthetic ideals
- Represents internalized moral standards
- Seeks polish, coherence, and alignment with rules
- **Purpose**: Ensure output has form, coherence, and integrity

### 3. **The Ego** – The Mediator/Editor
- Balances raw creativity with refined criticism
- Curates and reconciles contradictions
- Acts as human-like decision-maker
- Selects lines, rewrites, merges, and edits
- **Purpose**: Synthesize divergent inputs into unified output

---

1. **Id** generates first draft (wild, expressive)
2. **Superego** critiques and restructures with constraints
3. **Ego** evaluates both, creates merged version
4. Optional human final pass for voice and resonance

### Agent Dynamics
- Round-robin roles possible (agents switch functions)
- Use GPT + Claude (or similar) as different personas
- Optional third mediator agent ("Eliza"/"Sybiliza") to manage flow
- Script or automate the pipeline between models

Based on Freud's psyche model:
- **Id** = Impulse/Instinct (pleasure principle)

- **Superego** = Morality/Ideals (perfectionism)

## Why It Works

- Mirrors real creative collaboration (writer vs editor vs producer)

- Natural system design that feels useful
- "3 is the magic number" for balance

## Applications

- Poetry and songwriting
- Narrative writing and screenplays
- Design brainstorms
- Philosophical dialogues
- Debates or dual-perspective essays
- Any creative process needing iteration

- Progress to scripted conversation handling
- Balance opposing forces for synthesis
- Add human touch as final layer
- System can evolve from simple to complex
